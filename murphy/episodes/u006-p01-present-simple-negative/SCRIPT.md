# Unit 6 — complete editable narration script

Episode `u006-p01-present-simple-negative`. Silent visual draft v01; provisional 30 FPS scene and caption timings. Owner narration only. No audio generated.

All teaching and exercises are original. Bracketed cues are production directions, not spoken narration.

## 0–20s — From a habit to a negative

[Visual: visual-context; hook.]

In Unit 5, she drinks tea every morning describes a positive habit. Now the record changes: no tea. She doesn't drink tea. The negative says this habit is not true.

## 20–36s — A preference can be negative

[Visual: visual-context; real-life-example.]

A negative can describe a preference too. First, I enjoy seafood. Change the meaning: I don't enjoy seafood. We are saying that the positive statement is not true.

## 36–52s — An activity can be absent

[Visual: visual-context; real-life-example.]

Daniel drives. Now imagine Daniel has no driving habit. Daniel doesn't drive. The negative tells us the activity does not happen; it does not tell us why.

## 52–68s — A general fact

[Visual: visual-context; real-life-example.]

Negatives are useful for general facts as well. Fish don't have hands. We can make negative statements about habits, preferences, and facts, just as Unit 5 used positive statements.

## 68–84s — Two familiar contractions

[Visual: phrase-focus; visual-explanation.]

Don't means do not. The apostrophe marks the shorter form. Doesn't means does not. Both help us make ordinary present simple sentences negative.

## 84–104s — The negative pattern

[Visual: sentence-builder; visual-explanation.]

Put don't or doesn't after the subject, then use the base verb. Here, base verb means the plain form: drink, like, work. Do and does are helping verbs, also called auxiliaries.

## 104–120s — I and you: don’t

[Visual: sentence-builder; grammar-breakdown.]

With I, use don't: I don't eat meat. With you, use don't too: you don't need a ticket. You keeps this form for one person or several.

## 120–136s — We and they: don’t

[Visual: sentence-builder; grammar-breakdown.]

We don't live nearby. They don't watch television. I, you, we, and they all use don't. The main verb stays in its base form.

## 136–152s — He and she: doesn’t

[Visual: sentence-builder; grammar-breakdown.]

He doesn't drive. She doesn't eat seafood. With he and she, use doesn't. The helper changes to match the subject, just as the positive verb changed in Unit 5.

## 152–168s — It takes doesn’t

[Visual: sentence-builder; grammar-breakdown.]

Imagine a broken machine. It doesn't work. It belongs with he and she. The helper has two subject forms: don't with I, you, we, they; doesn't with he, she, it.

## 168–186s — Names and noun phrases

[Visual: visual-context; grammar-breakdown.]

A name follows the same pattern. Daniel can become he, so Daniel takes doesn't. My friends can become they, so my friends takes don't. Match the whole subject, not just a nearby word.

## 186–204s — One person or several

[Visual: sentence-builder; grammar-breakdown.]

Daniel doesn't drive. My friends don't drive. The activity word drive stays the same. Choosing don't or doesn't depends on the subject; being negative does not change which subject group it belongs to.

## 204–224s — Watch likes become like

[Visual: sentence-builder; grammar-breakdown.]

She likes chocolate. To make it negative, insert doesn't and change likes to like. She doesn't like chocolate. Doesn't already carries the form for she, so we do not mark the main verb with s again.

## 224–242s — One subject form is enough

[Visual: phrase-focus; grammar-breakdown.]

In the positive, likes has the s ending. In the negative, doesn't matches she. Keep like in the base form. The meaning is negative; the subject is still she.

## 242–258s — Remove the -es ending

[Visual: phrase-focus; grammar-breakdown.]

Watches has an es ending in the positive. After doesn't, use watch, not watches. She doesn't watch films. We change the form, not just add a negative word.

## 258–276s — Return to the whole base verb

[Visual: phrase-focus; grammar-breakdown.]

Studies is the positive form you learned in Unit 5. After doesn't, return to study, with y. We do not just remove the final s and leave studie.

## 276–292s — Avoid two subject markings

[Visual: comparison; grammar-breakdown.]

Look at the first sentence. Doesn't already matches she. Use the base verb work after it. Say: She doesn't work here.

## 292–310s — Has returns to have

[Visual: phrase-focus; grammar-breakdown.]

Has is the positive form with she. After doesn't, use have. She doesn't have a bicycle. The same base-verb rule applies to this special positive form.

## 310–330s — Two jobs for do

[Visual: phrase-focus; grammar-breakdown.]

He does his homework is positive. He doesn't do his homework is negative. Doesn't is the helper; do names the activity. These two words have different jobs.

## 330–348s — Goes returns to go

[Visual: phrase-focus; grammar-breakdown.]

She goes by train describes her travel habit. In the negative, goes becomes go: she doesn't go by train. After doesn't, use go, not goes.

## 348–364s — Be keeps its own negative

[Visual: phrase-focus; grammar-breakdown.]

Remember the earlier be pattern: she is ready. She isn't ready uses is not. With be, we do not add doesn't. Recognize the verb pattern before choosing the negative.

## 364–388s — Three familiar verb patterns

[Visual: sentence-builder; grammar-breakdown.]

He isn't tired describes a condition with be. He doesn't work today uses the ordinary verb work. He isn't working right now uses be with working, as in Unit 3. Recognize the pattern; a full tense comparison comes later.

## 388–406s — Choose from the verb pattern

[Visual: sentence-builder; grammar-breakdown.]

They don't work here, but they aren't ready. Work uses don't plus its base form. Ready describes them with be, so use aren't. Don't choose a negative only from the subject; check the verb pattern too.

## 406–424s — Never: no occasions

[Visual: visual-context; grammar-breakdown.]

Read my coffee record. No coffee on any day shown. I never drink coffee. Never means on no occasion, not just rarely. This picture illustrates my stated habit; it is not a fixed definition based on fourteen days.

## 424–442s — Not very often: occasionally

[Visual: visual-context; grammar-breakdown.]

Now coffee appears on two days. I don't drink coffee very often. In this example, I drink it occasionally. Not very often means infrequently; it does not say never. The exact number is only an illustration.

## 442–460s — These meanings are different

[Visual: visual-context; grammar-breakdown.]

Never rules the action out. Don't drink coffee very often leaves room for some coffee, as the second record shows. These sentences are not always equivalent. Use the wording that matches the actual habit.

## 460–478s — Usually in a negative

[Visual: sentence-builder; grammar-breakdown.]

She doesn't usually travel by train. Usually naturally comes after doesn't and before the main verb here. Train travel is not her normal pattern, but it can still happen sometimes. We are not saying never.

## 478–496s — Often in a negative

[Visual: sentence-builder; grammar-breakdown.]

They don't often visit the city. Often fits after don't here. Another natural pattern is I don't drink coffee very often, with very often at the end. These are useful patterns, not every possible position.

## 496–512s — Match he with doesn’t

[Visual: comparison; grammar-breakdown.]

Look at the first sentence. He takes doesn't. Like stays in its base form. Say: He doesn't like swimming.

## 512–528s — Match the whole subject

[Visual: comparison; grammar-breakdown.]

Look at the first sentence. My brother matches he, so use doesn't, then have. Say: My brother doesn't have a car.

## 528–544s — Have, not has

[Visual: comparison; grammar-breakdown.]

Look at the first sentence. After doesn't, use the base verb have. Has is the positive form. Say: He doesn't have a bicycle.

## 544–560s — They belongs with don’t

[Visual: comparison; grammar-breakdown.]

Look at the first sentence. They takes don't. Keep live in its base form. Say: They don't live here.

## 560–578s — Make the sentence negative

[Visual: quiz; guided-practice.]

I like cold soup. → I \_\_\_ cold soup. [Pause for five seconds.] don't like. I takes don't, followed by the base verb like.

## 578–596s — Choose the helper

[Visual: quiz; guided-practice.]

You \_\_\_ need a key. [Pause for five seconds.] don't. You takes don't, for one person or several.

## 596–614s — Match the named subject

[Visual: quiz; guided-practice.]

Asha \_\_\_ drive. [Pause for five seconds.] doesn't. Asha can become she, so use doesn't.

## 614–632s — Match the whole group

[Visual: quiz; guided-practice.]

My cousins \_\_\_ live nearby. [Pause for five seconds.] don't. My cousins can become they, so use don't.

## 632–650s — Return to the base verb

[Visual: quiz; guided-practice.]

He doesn't \_\_\_ films. [Pause for five seconds.] watch. Doesn't matches he; use watch without the es ending.

## 650–668s — Restore the y

[Visual: quiz; guided-practice.]

She doesn't \_\_\_ at night. [Pause for five seconds.] study. The base verb is study. Studies belongs in a positive sentence.

## 668–686s — Choose the negative verb

[Visual: quiz; guided-practice.]

Mia doesn't \_\_\_ a car. [Pause for five seconds.] have. After doesn't, use have, not has.

## 686–704s — One helper, one activity

[Visual: quiz; guided-practice.]

He doesn't \_\_\_ his homework here. [Pause for five seconds.] do. Doesn't is the helper; do is the base verb for the activity.

## 704–722s — Use the base travel verb

[Visual: quiz; guided-practice.]

She doesn't \_\_\_ by bus. [Pause for five seconds.] go. Go is the base verb. Don't keep goes after doesn't.

## 722–740s — Correct the main verb

[Visual: quiz; guided-practice.]

Revise: She doesn't works here. → She \_\_\_ here. [Pause for five seconds.] doesn't work. Doesn't matches she. Work must stay in its base form.

## 740–758s — Recognize the be pattern

[Visual: quiz; guided-practice.]

They \_\_\_ ready. [Pause for five seconds.] aren't. Ready goes with be here, so use aren't, not don't.

## 758–776s — Never or rarely?

[Visual: quiz; guided-practice.]

Coffee twice in 14 days: I \_\_\_. [Pause for five seconds.] don't drink it very often. Some coffee happens; never means none.

## 776–792s — Look at Lea’s drink record

[Visual: visual-context; guided-practice.]

Look at Lea and the crossed coffee symbol. The record says she drinks no coffee on any day. Use that information in the next exercise. We need a negative habit, not a statement about this moment.

## 792–810s — Describe Lea’s habit

[Visual: quiz; guided-practice.]

Lea: no coffee on any day. She \_\_\_ coffee. [Pause for five seconds.] doesn't drink. She takes doesn't, then the base verb drink. The visual shows no coffee.

## 810–830s — Keep these two patterns

[Visual: recap; recap.]

Use don't with I, you, we, and they. Use doesn't with he, she, and it. After either helper, use the base verb. Remember doesn't have, doesn't do, and doesn't go.

## 830–848s — One practical negative

[Visual: sentence-builder; recap.]

My sister doesn't drink coffee. My sister matches she, so choose doesn't. Then use the base verb drink. You can now say which habits, preferences, or facts are not true, using the correct negative pattern.
