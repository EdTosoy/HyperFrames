# Unit 7 — complete editable narration script

Episode `u007-p01-present-simple-questions`. Silent draft v01; provisional 30 FPS scene/caption timings. Owner narration only. All teaching and exercises are original. Bracketed cues are directions, not spoken narration.

## 0–22s — From telling to asking

[Visual: sentence-builder; hook.]

Unit 5: she plays tennis. Unit 6: she doesn't play tennis. Now ask: does she play tennis? Does comes before she. Like doesn't, does takes the base verb play. We are asking whether the habit is true.

## 22–38s — Ask about a preference

[Visual: visual-context; real-life-example.]

You are getting to know a new neighbour. You ask: do you enjoy hiking? You want to know about a general preference. You are not simply describing an action you can see right now.

## 38–54s — Ask about a shared need

[Visual: visual-context; real-life-example.]

At an entrance, you and a friend are unsure about tickets. Do we need tickets? Present simple questions can check a situation or fact, as well as habits and preferences.

## 54–74s — A question needs a helper

[Visual: sentence-builder; visual-explanation.]

You enjoy hiking tells something. Do you enjoy hiking asks whether it is true. Add do before you. Do is a helping verb here: it builds this ordinary present simple question. A question mark alone is not the pattern.

## 74–90s — Do with you and they

[Visual: sentence-builder; grammar-breakdown.]

Do you enjoy cooking? Do they live nearby? Use do with you and they. We are extending the subject groups you used with don't in Unit 6, so the agreement is familiar.

## 90–106s — Do with we and I

[Visual: sentence-builder; grammar-breakdown.]

Do we need tickets? Do I need to register? I, you, we, and they use do. I stays with this group even though it means one speaker.

## 106–122s — Does with she and it

[Visual: sentence-builder; grammar-breakdown.]

Does Emma speak French? Emma can become she, so use does. Does this machine work? This machine can become it. He, she, and it use does.

## 122–138s — Match the complete subject

[Visual: sentence-builder; grammar-breakdown.]

Does your brother like painting? Does your dog sleep indoors? Match the whole subject to its pronoun. Your brother takes does because he takes does. Your dog can match it.

## 138–154s — Names and plural groups

[Visual: visual-context; grammar-breakdown.]

The same agreement works with names and groups. Emma takes does. Your friends takes do because the phrase can become they. Do not choose the helper from the word your; consider the whole subject.

## 154–172s — Build the question in order

[Visual: sentence-builder; grammar-breakdown.]

Do your neighbours own a car? First the helper do, then the whole subject your neighbours, then the base verb own. Keep the remaining information after the verb. This order makes the standard yes or no question.

## 172–192s — Does carries the subject form

[Visual: sentence-builder; grammar-breakdown.]

He enjoys music becomes does he enjoy music? Does already carries the form for he, so enjoy stays in the base form. This is the same base-verb rule you used after doesn't. Do not mark the main verb again.

## 192–210s — works → work

[Visual: visual-context; grammar-breakdown.]

He works here. Watch the ending move away. Remove the positive ending s. After does, use the base verb work. Does he work here? Does comes before the subject; the base verb follows it.

## 210–228s — likes → like

[Visual: visual-context; grammar-breakdown.]

She likes music. Watch the ending move away. Remove the positive ending s. After does, use the base verb like. Does she like music? Does comes before the subject; the base verb follows it.

## 228–246s — watches → watch

[Visual: visual-context; grammar-breakdown.]

She watches films. Watch the ending move away. Remove the positive ending es. After does, use the base verb watch. Does she watch films? Does comes before the subject; the base verb follows it.

## 246–264s — studies → study

[Visual: visual-context; grammar-breakdown.]

He studies here. Watch the ending move away. Restore the whole base word study, with y. Do not leave the incomplete spelling studie. Does he study here? Does comes before the subject; the base verb follows it.

## 264–282s — has → have

[Visual: visual-context; grammar-breakdown.]

She has a bicycle. Watch the ending move away. Has returns to have. Use does she have, not does she has. Does she have a bicycle? Does comes before the subject; the base verb follows it.

## 282–300s — goes → go

[Visual: visual-context; grammar-breakdown.]

He goes by train. Watch the ending move away. Remove the positive ending es. After does, use the base verb go. Does he go by train? Does comes before the subject; the base verb follows it.

## 300–316s — Use like after does

[Visual: comparison; grammar-breakdown.]

Check the first question. Does matches she. Keep like in its base form, without s. Say: Does she like books?

## 316–334s — Yes/no or specific information?

[Visual: visual-context; grammar-breakdown.]

Does Lila study here can have a yes or no answer. Where does Lila study asks for a place instead. Question words tell the listener which information we need. The structure still includes does before the subject.

## 334–352s — What: ask about a thing

[Visual: visual-context; grammar-breakdown.]

What do you usually eat for breakfast? What asks for the food, not just yes or no. Eggs and toast is a possible answer. Put what before the familiar do you pattern.

## 352–370s — Where: ask about a place

[Visual: visual-context; grammar-breakdown.]

Where does Lila study? Where asks for a place. She studies at the library gives that information. In the question, keep does before Lila and use study. A full positive answer has its normal verb ending.

## 370–386s — When: ask about a time

[Visual: visual-context; grammar-breakdown.]

When do they finish work? When asks for a time. In this situation, they finish at five. The question starts when, then do, then they, then the base verb finish.

## 386–402s — Why: ask for a reason

[Visual: visual-context; grammar-breakdown.]

Why does Kai take the train? Why asks for a reason. A possible answer is because the journey is fast. Keep the main verb take in its base form after does and the subject Kai.

## 402–420s — How: ask about the method

[Visual: visual-context; grammar-breakdown.]

How does Noor travel to school? How asks about the method here. She travels by bicycle. The question uses does Noor travel; the positive answer uses she travels. Each structure keeps its own form.

## 420–438s — How often: ask about frequency

[Visual: visual-context; grammar-breakdown.]

How often does Maya visit her grandparents? How often asks about frequency. Her declared routine is a visit every Sunday. Put the whole phrase how often before does. Visit remains in its base form.

## 438–456s — How much: ask about a price

[Visual: visual-context; grammar-breakdown.]

How much does this notebook cost? Here, how much asks for the price. The example notebook costs four dollars. Does matches this notebook, and cost stays in the base form in the question.

## 456–476s — Keep the familiar question inside

[Visual: sentence-builder; grammar-breakdown.]

Put the question word before the do or does question. It names the information you want: where for a place, when for a time, why for a reason. These are the standard patterns we are practising, not a rule for every English question.

## 476–496s — A usual weekly routine

[Visual: visual-context; grammar-breakdown.]

Follow Leo's declared weekday exercise routine. Does Leo usually exercise on weekdays? Usually comes after the subject Leo and before exercise. The picture helps explain the habit; it does not give a fixed numerical definition of usually.

## 496–514s — Frequency after the subject

[Visual: sentence-builder; grammar-breakdown.]

Do you usually exercise in the morning? Does he always arrive early? In these questions, usually and always come after the subject and before the main verb. The helper still comes first.

## 514–532s — Often and normally

[Visual: sentence-builder; grammar-breakdown.]

Do they often visit their friends? What do you normally do after school? Normally means usually here. The frequency word follows the subject. What stays before the helping do; the final do names the activity.

## 532–548s — Keep usually after he

[Visual: comparison; grammar-breakdown.]

Check the first question. Put the subject he before usually; keep work in the base form. Say: Does he usually work on Saturdays?

## 548–564s — Meeting someone new

[Visual: visual-context; grammar-breakdown.]

In a conversation with someone you have just met, what do you do commonly asks about their job. Nia answers: I'm a software engineer. The context helps us understand what the speaker means.

## 564–582s — Ask about someone’s work

[Visual: dialogue; grammar-breakdown.]

What do you do commonly asks about a job. I'm a software engineer is a natural answer here. It does not describe what Nia is doing at this exact second.

## 582–602s — Two words, different jobs

[Visual: sentence-builder; grammar-breakdown.]

In what do you do, the first do is the helping verb. It forms the question before you. The second do is the main verb. Here it refers to the person's work. The words look alike, but their jobs differ.

## 602–620s — Context can change the meaning

[Visual: dialogue; grammar-breakdown.]

With after class, what do you do asks about an activity in a routine, not a job. Context matters. The same helping do and main verb do still have different jobs.

## 620–638s — A name becomes a pronoun

[Visual: dialogue; grammar-breakdown.]

Your brother becomes he in the reply. Yes, he does answers the question without repeating drive. Match the answer subject to the person being asked about.

## 638–658s — The speaker’s viewpoint

[Visual: dialogue; grammar-breakdown.]

The question says you because it addresses the listener. The listener answers I when talking about themselves: yes, I do. Keep do; yes, I am belongs to a be question instead.

## 658–676s — Positive answers with do

[Visual: phrase-focus; grammar-breakdown.]

Positive short answers use yes, a subject, and the matching helper. Yes, I do. Yes, you do. Yes, we do. Yes, they do. Choose the pronoun from who is speaking and who the question is about.

## 676–694s — Negative answers with don’t

[Visual: phrase-focus; grammar-breakdown.]

For a negative answer, use the matching negative helper. No, I don't. No, you don't. No, we don't. No, they don't. The action phrase does not need to be repeated.

## 694–718s — Answers with does

[Visual: sentence-builder; grammar-breakdown.]

With he, she, or it, the short answer uses does or doesn't. Yes, he does; no, he doesn't. The same pattern gives yes, she does, and no, she doesn't; yes, it does, and no, it doesn't.

## 718–736s — A plural subject in the reply

[Visual: dialogue; grammar-breakdown.]

Your friends becomes they. Do your friends walk here? No, they don't. Use the matching negative helper and leave out walk here because the question already supplies it.

## 736–754s — Remember the be pattern

[Visual: sentence-builder; grammar-breakdown.]

Are you tired uses be directly, as in Unit 2. Do you feel tired uses the ordinary verb feel. Match the helper to the verb pattern, not just the subject. Both questions are natural, but their structures differ.

## 754–772s — Keep the learned structures

[Visual: sentence-builder; grammar-breakdown.]

Are you studying uses be with verb ing, as in Unit 4. Do you study every evening asks about a routine with the ordinary verb study. Keep the patterns distinct. The full tense comparison belongs in Unit 8.

## 772–788s — Match he with does

[Visual: comparison; grammar-breakdown.]

Check the first question. He takes does. Work stays in the base form. Say: Does he work here?

## 788–804s — Keep the helper after where

[Visual: comparison; grammar-breakdown.]

Check the first question. Where requests a place; then use does, she and the base verb live. Say: Where does she live?

## 804–820s — Match the plural subject

[Visual: comparison; grammar-breakdown.]

Check the first question. Your friends matches they, so choose do. Say: Do your friends play football?

## 820–836s — Have after does

[Visual: comparison; grammar-breakdown.]

Check the first question. Does matches she. Use have, not has, as the main verb. Say: Does she have a bicycle?

## 836–854s — Choose the helper

[Visual: quiz; guided-practice.]

blank your aunt teach here? [Pause for five seconds.] Does. Your aunt matches she, so choose does.

## 854–872s — Match a plural subject

[Visual: quiz; guided-practice.]

blank your cousins live nearby? [Pause for five seconds.] Do. Your cousins matches they, so choose do.

## 872–890s — Turn a statement into a question

[Visual: quiz; guided-practice.]

Ava cooks. Turn this statement into a yes or no question. [Pause for five seconds.] Does Ava cook? Does comes before Ava; cook is the base verb.

## 890–908s — Choose the base verb

[Visual: quiz; guided-practice.]

Does he blank films at home? [Pause for five seconds.] watch. After does, use watch without es.

## 908–926s — Use have after does

[Visual: quiz; guided-practice.]

Does Sam blank a bicycle? [Pause for five seconds.] have. Sam matches he or she. Use does and the base verb have.

## 926–944s — Put the words in order

[Visual: quiz; guided-practice.]

Order: work / does / he / here [Pause for five seconds.] Does he work here? Put does before he, then the base verb work.

## 944–962s — Ask for a place

[Visual: quiz; guided-practice.]

blank does Tom live? A place. [Pause for five seconds.] Where. Where requests a place; keep does Tom live.

## 962–980s — Ask about a price

[Visual: quiz; guided-practice.]

blank does this pen cost? [Pause for five seconds.] How much. How much requests the price here.

## 980–998s — Place usually naturally

[Visual: quiz; guided-practice.]

Choose the usual word order. [Pause for five seconds.] Does she usually walk? The subject she comes before usually.

## 998–1016s — Answer from your viewpoint

[Visual: quiz; guided-practice.]

Do you cycle? Answer yes. [Pause for five seconds.] Yes, I do. The listener uses I for themselves; keep do.

## 1016–1034s — Answer about a third person

[Visual: quiz; guided-practice.]

Does your brother cook? Answer no. [Pause for five seconds.] No, he doesn't. Your brother becomes he; use the negative helper.

## 1034–1050s — Read Noor’s routine

[Visual: visual-context; guided-practice.]

Noor's usual journey is by bicycle. In the next exercise, ask about her travel method. Use a question word, the matching helper, and the base verb. Keep the whole situation in mind.

## 1050–1068s — Ask about the method

[Visual: quiz; guided-practice.]

Ask about Noor's method of travel. Make a question starting with how. [Pause for five seconds.] How does Noor travel to school? How requests the method; use does Noor plus base travel.

## 1068–1086s — Keep the question structure

[Visual: recap; recap.]

Use do with I, you, we, and they. Use does with he, she, and it. Put the helper before the subject and use the base main verb. Does already carries the third-person form.

## 1086–1106s — Build on that structure

[Visual: recap; recap.]

For the patterns taught here, put the question word before do or does. Usually, always, and often commonly follow the subject. Give short answers with do, does, don't, or doesn't. Let us finish with one everyday conversation.

## 1106–1122s — One everyday conversation

[Visual: dialogue; recap.]

Does your sister usually cook? Yes, she does. Your sister matches she, usually follows the subject, and cook stays in its base form.

## 1122–1140s — Ask for more information

[Visual: dialogue; recap.]

What does she cook? She often makes soup. What asks for the food. The question uses does she cook, and the positive answer returns to makes. You can now ask and answer about everyday routines.
