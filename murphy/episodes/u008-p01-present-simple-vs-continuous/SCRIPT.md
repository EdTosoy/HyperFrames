# Unit 8 — complete editable voice-over script

Episode `u008-p01-present-simple-vs-continuous`. Silent draft v01. All scene/caption times are provisional on the 30 FPS grid. Owner narration only. Bracketed cues are directions, not spoken narration. All teaching and exercises are original.

## 0–18s — One person, one activity

[Visual: visual-context; hook.]

Meet Maya. Watch the page move while she reads her novel. She is reading a novel. The present continuous presents the activity in progress. Keep Maya and her book in mind for the next view.

## 18–36s — Now look across her evenings

[Visual: visual-context; real-life-example.]

Here is the same Maya and the same reading activity across her usual evenings. She reads every evening. The present simple describes a repeated habit. It does not tell us whether her book is open at this exact moment.

## 36–56s — Both can be true

[Visual: sentence-builder; real-life-example.]

Maya can be reading while she also has a regular reading habit. Both sentences can be true. The choice depends on the view we want to give: the activity in progress, or the usual pattern. Both refer to present time.

## 56–76s — Around now, not every second

[Visual: visual-context; visual-explanation.]

Maya's reading project is in progress this week. This minute she is taking a short break. She can still say she is reading a long novel. Continuous does not always mean an action at the exact second of speaking.

## 76–94s — An unfinished current activity

[Visual: timeline; visual-explanation.]

This line shows the reading project around now. Some reading is before this moment, and there are more pages to read. A short break does not end the whole project. We are describing the current activity, not a future arrangement.

## 94–112s — An action we can watch

[Visual: visual-context; visual-explanation.]

The pen moves across the message. He is writing; he isn't sleeping. These describe what is happening, and what is not happening, in the situation we are watching. You already know this continuous pattern from Unit 3.

## 112–128s — A phone conversation

[Visual: visual-context; visual-explanation.]

She is talking on the phone. We present the conversation as an activity in progress. Listen to the meaning first; the familiar is plus talking form follows that meaning.

## 128–146s — A job can be true away from work

[Visual: visual-context; visual-explanation.]

Her job is at a hospital, but the picture places her at home with a book. She works at a hospital is still true. Present simple gives a general fact about her job, not necessarily an activity happening before our eyes.

## 146–164s — Basketball in progress

[Visual: visual-context; visual-explanation.]

Watch the ball move between the players. They are playing basketball. We are showing the game as an activity in progress. The sentence alone does not tell us their usual weekend routine.

## 164–182s — The same game as a habit

[Visual: visual-context; visual-explanation.]

Now view the same players and the same game across their declared weekends. They play basketball every weekend. This tells us their repeated pattern. They may be doing something else at the moment we say it.

## 182–200s — Dinner is being prepared

[Visual: visual-context; visual-explanation.]

Watch the spoon move in the bowl. She is cooking dinner. The cooking is in progress. The picture gives us that situation; we do not need to find a special time word before choosing the continuous.

## 200–218s — Most evenings, a usual pattern

[Visual: visual-context; visual-explanation.]

The same cook and kitchen now appear in a record of her normal evenings. She cooks dinner most evenings. We are describing a routine, not claiming that she is beside the bowl at every moment. The counts are an illustration.

## 218–236s — A present work period

[Visual: visual-context; visual-explanation.]

Imagine this is your home workspace during today's work period. I'm working from home today presents work in progress around today. It does not, by itself, tell someone your usual work arrangement.

## 236–254s — A usual work arrangement

[Visual: visual-context; visual-explanation.]

I work from home describes the usual arrangement. It does not mean I am working every minute. Both forms can fit the same person: one focuses on current work, and the other on the general pattern.

## 254–270s — Generally true

[Visual: visual-context; visual-explanation.]

Present simple is useful for general facts too. Birds have feathers. This is a general description, not an activity that starts while we watch. We will also use the simple form for common preferences and states.

## 270–286s — A general negative habit

[Visual: visual-context; visual-explanation.]

He doesn't drink coffee describes his general habit. It does not tell us what drink is in his hand this second. The sentence is about his usual life, just as a positive present simple sentence can be.

## 286–306s — Retrieve the familiar forms

[Visual: sentence-builder; grammar-breakdown.]

I'm preparing lunch presents an activity in progress. I prepare lunch every morning presents a routine. You know the forms already: be plus verb ing for continuous, and the ordinary present simple verb for the usual pattern. Let meaning guide the choice.

## 306–326s — Ask about action or habit

[Visual: sentence-builder; grammar-breakdown.]

Are you working right now asks about the activity in progress. Do you work on weekends asks about a regular schedule. Use the question structure that matches your meaning. We are combining the patterns from Units 4 and 7.

## 326–344s — Two questions about study

[Visual: sentence-builder; grammar-breakdown.]

Is she studying? Does she study every evening? The first asks about study in progress; the second about a habit. Keep studying with is, but the base verb study after does and the subject.

## 344–362s — Two questions about playing

[Visual: sentence-builder; grammar-breakdown.]

Are they playing asks about a game in progress. Do they play regularly asks about a repeated activity. A yes answer to one question does not automatically answer the other. Listen to what the speaker wants to know.

## 362–378s — Not watching in this situation

[Visual: visual-context; grammar-breakdown.]

Here, the television is off and you are reading. I'm not watching television says the viewing activity is not happening. It does not say that you never watch television as a general habit.

## 378–396s — Not watching very often

[Visual: visual-context; grammar-breakdown.]

This record shows occasional television viewing. I don't watch television very often describes a general frequency. Some viewing is possible, as Unit 6 explained. This differs from saying the activity is not happening in a particular situation.

## 396–412s — Not driving at this moment

[Visual: visual-context; grammar-breakdown.]

Here is Dan, relaxing with a book. He isn't driving now describes his current situation. It gives us no complete answer about how he usually travels to work.

## 412–430s — A usual journey without driving

[Visual: visual-context; grammar-breakdown.]

Dan's declared weekday journey is by train. He doesn't drive to work describes that routine. Both this sentence and he isn't driving now may be true, but they tell us different things.

## 430–448s — Keep each negative pattern

[Visual: sentence-builder; grammar-breakdown.]

Keep not with the form of be in the continuous. Keep don't or doesn't with a base verb in ordinary present simple negatives. Meaning chooses the pattern; the pattern then gives you the correct verb form.

## 448–468s — A new idea: states

[Visual: visual-context; grammar-breakdown.]

Some verbs describe a state: what someone knows, wants, feels, or believes. I know the answer describes knowledge. It can be true right now without being an activity performed in progress. In this ordinary meaning, use present simple.

## 468–484s — Know is a state here

[Visual: comparison; grammar-breakdown.]

Check the first question. Know describes knowledge in this ordinary meaning, so use the simple form. Say: I know the answer.

## 484–504s — Want and need

[Visual: visual-context; grammar-breakdown.]

She wants some water. We need some water. These describe a want or a need, even when they are true at this moment. Normally say I want a drink, not I'm wanting a drink, for the meaning we are practising.

## 504–522s — Like and love

[Visual: visual-context; grammar-breakdown.]

I like this story. I love this book. These ordinary meanings describe attitudes or feelings, not an activity we present as unfolding. Use the simple form here, even if you are expressing the feeling right now.

## 522–540s — Understand the meaning

[Visual: visual-context; grammar-breakdown.]

We understand the instructions says that their meaning is clear to us. It does not mean that we are performing the instructions at this moment. Understand normally takes the simple form for this state.

## 540–558s — Believe a person’s words

[Visual: dialogue; grammar-breakdown.]

I believe you means I accept what you say as true. In this ordinary meaning, believe describes a state of belief. Use the simple form, even though the belief is true now.

## 558–578s — Remember and forget

[Visual: visual-context; grammar-breakdown.]

I remember her name describes what is in my memory. I often forget names describes a repeated memory habit. Use the simple form in these examples. We are not making a rule about every possible use of remember or forget.

## 578–596s — Prefer: a state of preference

[Visual: visual-context; grammar-breakdown.]

He prefers tea describes his preference between drinks. It does not say he is drinking tea while we speak. Prefer normally uses the simple form for this ordinary meaning, like the other state meanings we are learning.

## 596–614s — Mean: what a sign expresses

[Visual: visual-context; grammar-breakdown.]

This sign means stop. Mean describes what the sign expresses; it is not an activity the sign is performing. Use means for this ordinary meaning. A state can be true in the present without needing a continuous form.

## 614–634s — Usually, not never

[Visual: phrase-focus; grammar-breakdown.]

These verbs generally use present simple for the ordinary meanings taught here. Do not turn that into a claim that they can never be continuous. Some have other meanings or contexts with continuous forms; those uses are outside this beginner lesson.

## 634–654s — Have can describe different things

[Visual: visual-context; grammar-breakdown.]

I'm having lunch describes an activity, so having is natural. I have a bicycle describes possession, so use have in this ordinary meaning. The same verb can have different meanings. Choose the form from the meaning, not just the word have.

## 654–674s — Describe her ordinary schedule

[Visual: comparison; grammar-breakdown.]

The intended meaning is her ordinary, regular schedule. Prefer she works every Sunday. The continuous wording can fit other contexts; we are not claiming it is impossible in every situation. Here we want the usual pattern.

## 674–690s — Keep the base verb after doesn’t

[Visual: comparison; grammar-breakdown.]

Check the first question. After doesn't, use the base verb work, as in Unit 6. Say: He doesn't work here.

## 690–706s — Keep -ing after are

[Visual: comparison; grammar-breakdown.]

Check the first question. This question asks about an activity in progress; are takes verb-ing. Say: Are you working now?

## 706–722s — Keep study after does

[Visual: comparison; grammar-breakdown.]

Check the first question. For this routine question, use does and the base verb study. Say: Does she study every night?

## 722–736s — Look before choosing

[Visual: visual-context; guided-practice.]

Look at Maya and the book page. Use this situation in the next exercise. Think about what the picture lets you describe, then choose the form that fits.

## 736–754s — Choose from the visible action

[Visual: quiz; guided-practice.]

The book page turns as we watch. She blank. [Pause for five seconds.] is reading. The picture presents reading as an activity in progress.

## 754–768s — Now view the declared routine

[Visual: visual-context; guided-practice.]

Now study Maya's declared evening record. Use the repeated occasions in this picture for the next exercise. The question will keep this context in words while you think.

## 768–786s — Describe the repeated activity

[Visual: quiz; guided-practice.]

Maya's usual evenings each show a book. She blank. [Pause for five seconds.] reads. This is her repeated habit, not a claim about this second.

## 786–804s — Can both sentences be true?

[Visual: quiz; guided-practice.]

She is reading. She reads regularly. blank [Pause for five seconds.] Both can be true. One describes an activity in progress; the other a habit.

## 804–822s — Choose the current negative

[Visual: quiz; guided-practice.]

You are reading; the TV is off. I blank TV. [Pause for five seconds.] am not watching. This describes the activity not happening in this situation.

## 822–840s — Ask about action

[Visual: quiz; guided-practice.]

A spoon moves in the soup. Ask about this action. [Pause for five seconds.] Is she cooking? Ask about an activity in progress with is plus verb-ing.

## 840–858s — Ask about a routine

[Visual: quiz; guided-practice.]

Ask about Lea’s regular Sunday game. [Pause for five seconds.] Does Lea play on Sundays? A regular schedule uses does with the base verb play.

## 858–876s — A state can be true now

[Visual: quiz; guided-practice.]

The answer is in my memory. I blank it. [Pause for five seconds.] know. Know describes knowledge in this ordinary meaning.

## 876–894s — A current want

[Visual: quiz; guided-practice.]

I feel thirsty. I blank some water. [Pause for five seconds.] want. Want normally uses the simple form for this state.

## 894–912s — Memory as a habit

[Visual: quiz; guided-practice.]

My memory often fails with names. I blank them. [Pause for five seconds.] forget. This describes a repeated habit of forgetting names.

## 912–930s — Having or owning?

[Visual: quiz; guided-practice.]

This bicycle belongs to me. I blank a bicycle. [Pause for five seconds.] have. Have describes possession here, so use the simple form.

## 930–948s — Repair the negative

[Visual: quiz; guided-practice.]

Repair: He doesn't working here. He doesn't blank here. [Pause for five seconds.] work. After doesn't, the main verb is the base form work.

## 948–966s — Repair the question

[Visual: quiz; guided-practice.]

Repair: Are you work now? Are you blank now? [Pause for five seconds.] working. Use are with verb-ing for this activity in progress.

## 966–984s — Explain the difference

[Visual: quiz; guided-practice.]

I work from home describes my blank. [Pause for five seconds.] usual arrangement. It does not say I am working at this exact moment.

## 984–1002s — Around now allows a break

[Visual: quiz; guided-practice.]

Must the present continuous always describe the exact second of speaking? [Pause for five seconds.] No. A current project can be in progress during a short break.

## 1002–1020s — Choose the meaning first

[Visual: recap; recap.]

Choose the meaning first. Present continuous presents an activity in progress, now or around now. Present simple presents habits, general facts, and common states. The same person and activity can fit both views.

## 1020–1040s — Meaning and form

[Visual: recap; recap.]

Keep each question and negative pattern with its tense. Be goes with verb ing in the continuous; do and does go with base verbs in ordinary simple questions and negatives. Know, want, and understand normally use simple forms for these ordinary states.

## 1040–1056s — One final real-life view

[Visual: visual-context; recap.]

Watch Nia's pen move as she writes to her sister. Nia is writing to her sister presents this activity in progress. Now change the view from this activity to the regular pattern.

## 1056–1074s — The same activity, a wider view

[Visual: visual-context; recap.]

Nia writes to her sister on Sundays describes her regular habit. The activity is the same; the meaning is different. Ask whether you want to describe what is in progress, or what is generally true, and choose the matching form.
